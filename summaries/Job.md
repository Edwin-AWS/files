# Job

## Introduction

### Title and Authorship

Though the book of Job pays no specific attention to authorial claims, many students of the book have long claimed that it is the work of Moses, considered to be the author of the Bible's founding documents. There is ample reason to associate the book with Moses as it seems to belong to the earliest stages of biblical composition.

### Date

Proposed dates of composition range across a span of more than a millennium, from the second millennium BC to the times of the post-exilic temple. Despite competing recommendations, internal evidence supports an early date for the setting of the story: the fact that Job offered his own sacrifice implies that the priesthood had not been established, and a temple had not been built (Job 1:5).

### Background

The book rolls back a veil to grant to humans some insights into the workings of the great controversy between good and evil.

### Theology and purpose

Despite its clear focus on one individual named Job, the book really discusses the integrity of all God's intelligent creation. In it, God's adversary argues that the relationship between God and his people is in disinterested love and that, therefore, it lacks integrity. The book of Job is one of the Bible's most explicit exhibitions of the adversary's character and role.

## Job 1 — Satan Challenges Job's Integrity

### Summary

Job was blameless. The LORD allowed Satan to test him. Job's servants and children were killed. He tore his robes and worshipped.

### Key Lessons

**1. Job introduced vs 1-3**

- Job is introduced as a wealthy person, listing first his spiritual wealth, followed by the richness of having a family, and finally a list of all his material possessions.
- Job feared God and shunned evil. The term blameless or complete – highlights his reliance on God for his righteousness. Job was also an upright person, meaning that he acted according to moral principles when dealing with others.
- Consequently, he avoided sin by not exposing himself to it — rather, he walked away from it.

**2. God's Court and the cosmic conflict 6-12**

- No reason is given for Satan's presence in the heavenly assembly. When given the opportunity to speak, Satan accused God of feeding selfishness into the creatures by rewarding good behavior.
- Satan was suggesting that God's way of governing simply bought the services of humans. Therefore, their love for Him was motivated by greed that was fed by God himself.

**3. Satan's first blow vs 13-22**

- Horror built up to its climax. Job was not allowed to recover as the rhythm went from lesser to greater, from lesser sorrow to most unbearable grief.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Lord help us understand the reality and effects of the Great Controversy. Amen.


## Job 2 — Satan Strikes Job's Health

### Summary

Satan struck Job with boils. Job's wife told him to curse God. But Job did not sin. Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar came to comfort him.

### Key Lessons

**1. The heavenly court: Presumption's persistence (v 1-6)**

- People will give everything they have to stay alive. Satan sought to make the case that grave sickness, perhaps perceived as a mortal threat, would be sufficient to expose Job's insincerity and separate him from his God.
- Allowing the adversary to torment his children would require him to take the blame for whatever cruelties would result from the adversary's aggression. Nevertheless, God made clear that Job's pain was inflicted by a hand other than His.

**2. Satan's second series: Sores and Spouse (v 7-10)**

- Mrs. Job apparently reasoned that life with God should consist of nothing but prosperity. Her husband judged her comment as born of folly rather than wisdom.
- Job's wife echoed Satan's taunts, and they decried it as folly. She was like a foolish woman, not that she was a foolish woman.

**3. Verdict on Job and the arrival of his friends (v 10-12)**

- Job was willing to administer for the Lord not only the good things he gave him but also the pain and suffering that had come his way.
- His relationship with the Lord was based on disinterested love. Then friends arrived.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Dear Lord, help us to hold on to you in times of prosperity and poverty, in Jesus' name we pray. Amen.


## Job 3 — Job Curses the Day of His Birth

### Summary

Job said, "Curse the day I was born! Why did I not die at birth? Why is light given to him who suffers? I have no rest, only turmoil."

### Key Lessons

**1. Longing for oblivion (v 1-10)**

- Jobs speech began with a curse, not upon the God who gives and takes away but against the day he was born.
- His unrealistic longing for reversal (v 7) indicates the depth of his frustration with existence.
- If his birth date could not be erased from the calendar, then it should be a night of no deliveries; if none of its births count Job would never have been – and he would not now be suffering.

**2. Death idealized (v 11-26)**

- Job lamented that he was not stillborn and that he coveted death's serenity.
- He knew that it is an oblivion where there are no varying of human status the righteous, wicked, slave, free are all equal.
- Although Job would have welcomed death, there is no indication that he ever considered ending his life by his own hand.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Dear Lord may your name be praised, even in difficult times may you remain Lord in our hearts and speech. Amen


## Job 4 — Eliphaz's Vision in the Night

### Summary

Eliphaz said, "Will you become impatient? When did the innocent ever perish? I heard a voice: 'Can a man be more righteous than God?'"

### Key Lessons

**1. The Vision Dream of Eliphaz - A personal revelatory Experience.**

- Eliphaz argued that the trustworthiness of his wisdom is unquestionable because it was grounded on a personal supernatural experience he had with a spiritual being who spoke to him.
- The content of the revelation will establish the identity of his being.

**2. Question**

- Can a human be considered to be righteous and morally upright before God?
- According to the spirit that spoke to Eliphaz, the natural distance between God and humans is such that this is impossible. The spirit applied this judgment.

**3. The identity of the Spirit**

- Based on the previous discussion, we suggest that Eliphaz is echoing the voice of the adversary from the prologue.
- Satan claimed that it was impossible for a human being to be righteous unless prompted by selfish motives.
- Second, it also contradicted God by arguing that humans suffer by divine design.
- Although Eliphaz did not say anything about the nature of the spirit that spoke to him, we can now ascertain without doubt that it was a messenger of the adversary. Eliphaz's statement shows that the adversary, though unseen, continues to present and prosecute his goal of pressing Job to curse God.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Dear Lord, help us to notice the workings of the surrounding devil. Like Job, help us to stand for you all the time. In Jesus' name we pray. Amen.


## Job 5 — Eliphaz Urges Job to Seek God

### Summary

"Who will answer you? Man is born to trouble. As for me, I would seek God. Do not despise his discipline. He wounds, but he binds up."

### Key Lessons

**1. Eliphaz: Job is chastened by God**

- Verses 2-7 emphasize his point regarding human inadequacy. Note that labels for the responsible one, such as foolish and simple, are typical of wisdom literature. Observe as well the vigor of Eliphaz's opposition to those labeled.

**2. Born of trouble Verse 7**

- Eliphaz was, in a sense, correct in affirming that humans were born of trouble, but if the natural lot of humanity were in trouble, how could he argue that Job's trouble was proof of specific wickedness?
- In verses 8-16, Eliphaz submitted an exceptional change in tone after his categorical denunciations.
- Job might yet have hope. He should turn to God because God is powerful beyond human understanding. He rules nature and is able to reverse anyone's circumstances.

**3. Soothing consolation**

- In an opening speech that had included reproof, revelation, and harsh contempt, Eliphaz moved on to soothing consolation (v 17-21).
- Problematic on the whole was not the general truth of divine chastening but his specific judgment that God was punishing Job (v 13-14, 17-19.)

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Dear Lord, we thank you for your words. May the study of today's chapter help us to understand you better. In Jesus' name we pray. Amen.


## Job 6 — Job Pleads for Understanding

### Summary

Job said, "My misery would outweigh the sand of the seas! You have proved no help. Show me how I have been wrong! Would I lie to you?"

### Key Lessons

**1. Job: Pity of himself**

- Frequently through the dialogue, Job longed to know he was appreciated.
- Living in uninterrupted agony, Job insisted that he had been faithful to God. But Job was not superhuman, verse 11-13. He saw no sense in going on; staying alive was to suffer.
- Job believed the loyalty of friends should survive spiritual crisis.

**2. Dismay at His Friends**

- Job's friends had treacherously disappointed him in their pretense of refreshing his spirit.
- Verse 20 is evidently descriptive of Job's own feelings as a parallel to those of travelers. Job was saying his friends are like those dried-up brooks whose water evaporates in the heat; they shrunk before a challenge.

**3. Job dared them to silence him**

- In verses 22–23, he becomes more assertive to his friends to show him his wrongs. They should just speak the truth rather than coerce.
- Job accused them of mercenary and exploitative conduct, a readiness to do the same to a friend to one they should instead loyally support.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Thank you dear Lord, for Jesus, our all-weather friend, a true, loyal, and supportive friend. Amen.


## Job 7 — Job's Burdensome Life

### Summary

"O God, my life is but a breath! Therefore, I will speak out. What is man, that you examine him? Why have you set me as your target?"

### Key Lessons

**1. Job's burdensome life (Verse 1-7)**

- Chapter 7 introduces the theme of Job's burdensome life. Job longed for some respite from days of stress and nights of tossing. But he had to confront the finality of the grave, verse 7-10, anticipated though not idealized as in chapter 3.
- While verse 9-10 might seem to imply that Job did not believe in a resurrection, this is not obvious from the text, especially in light of his later affirmation (Job 19:23-27).
- He was simply affirming in poetically suggestive language that the dead would not go back to their homes.
- Given the immanence of death, he would speak his mind. He preferred death to his current pains ; moreover, given the torments of life, Job preferred death.

**2. Need for forgiveness (verse 21)**

- Job recognized his need for forgiveness of sin, but he was unaware of a specific sin (verse 20) that would have caused God to punish so severely.
- He wished to die in peace knowing he was right with God. Should I die, am I right with God?

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Dear Lord, may you forgive our sins and help us to live uprightly. In Jesus' name we pray, Amen.


## Job 8 — Bildad Appeals to Tradition

### Summary

Bildad said, "Does God pervert justice? Learn from past generations. He will not reject the blameless, nor will he uphold evildoers."

### Key Lessons

**1. Bildad typical and limited Wisdom**

- Bildad believed that fate showed a reasonable God (v 1–7).
- Of the 3 friends, he is most faithful to radical wisdom traditions and most loyal to orthodox theology (i.e. reward for good and retribution for evil).
- Bildad's speeches were not characterized by Zophar's cruelty or Eliphaz's self-centeredness; he held to the views of wisdom. He took the argument to the level of family and suggested that God had killed Job's children because of their sin.
- Unlike Eliphaz, Bildad is grounded in reason rather than revelation, using the principle of retribution to establish that each one pays for his or her own sins and this includes children paying for their own sins.
- Bildad suggested that God would restore Job if he was or became pure and upright (verse 6).
- He used further conditional expressions (verse 4–5) to appeal to him with the promise of restoration, emphasizing the wisdom of previous generations.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Thank you Lord for your Word. May we trust in your wisdom and not in our own understanding. In Jesus' name. Amen.


## Job 9 — Job Longs for a Mediator

### Summary

Job said, "How can a man dispute with God? His power is vast! But he destroys the innocent. If only there were a mediator between us!"

### Key Lessons

**1. Job: Admiration of God**

- Job highlighted God's unmatched prowess in nature, in the opening lines of his second speech (v 1-12).
- The question of how a person can be righteous before God is similar to the one raised by Eliphaz's vision-dream, but the concern is a different one.

**2. Case with God**

- Job is using a different preposition, not "before God" but "with God"; that is to say, Job is asking whether it is possible for anyone to engage God in a legal dispute and win a case (be declared righteous vs 3).
- The implication is that in such a legal controversy, Job would lose the case even though he was righteous (v 15, 20).
- Verses 13-19 describe Job's sense of inadequacy before the Divine assaults.
- Job was fearful of his sufferings (v 28) – he feared to be optimistic lest he be proved wrong by suffering.
- By saying that God was not human like he was (v 32), Job was acknowledging the great distance separating him and all humans from God.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our heavenly Father. We thank you for your grace, love and unmatched prowess in nature. In Jesus' name, Amen.


## Job 10 — Job Questions Why He Was Born

### Summary

"God, why do you reject the work of your hands? You know that I am not guilty! Why did you bring me out of the womb? Leave me alone."

### Key Lessons

**1. Job speaks to God (v 1-7)**

- Job chose to complain and express how he felt as he continued to speak to God. He was not describing how God is but seeking to understand why he was experiencing so much pain.
- Hence, Job challenged God to justify what he was experiencing (v 2) and accused Him through a series of rhetorical questions of seemingly behaving like a flawed human (v 3-5), finding guilt were there was none (v 6-7).
- Then Job contrasted God's previous protection and support with what appeared to Job as wrath and condemnation (v 8-17).
- God who formed his embryo (v 10), brought it to maturity (v 11), and providentially cared for him (v 12), seems to Job, to have harbored ill intent against him all along (v 13-17).
- The end of v 17 has been translated variously as changes and war, wave upon wave of forces, fresh troops or hardships after hardships, can also be translated as hard service.
- In v 17 Job seems to be saying that he was constantly being called upon to render more hard service.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our Father in heaven, we thank you for your protection. Help us to understand you better, in Jesus' name. Amen


## Job 11 — Zophar Calls Job to Repent

### Summary

Zophar said, "Should your babble go unanswered? God exacts less than your guilt deserves! Reach out to him and you will find hope."

### Key Lessons

**1. Zophar: Apparent grace**

- Zophar was the least sensitive of the friends and least deferential to Job.
- He launched a brutal attack on Job (v 1-6) and returned to the question of 4:17 and 9:2: Can a human being be righteous?
- Zophar argued that a revelation from God would condemn Job (v 5). God, for Zophar, can only condemn humans as Eliphaz's spirit argued.
- Zophar had continued insisting that empty talk would not vindicate anybody.
- He said that Job had mocked but it is not clear when such a thing took place. Since Bildad, Job had spoken of God's power and Job's impotence before it, lamented his life and longed again for death.

**2. Zophar spoke of hope in repentance in his final segment (v 13-20)**

- Like Eliphaz and Bildad, he assured Job that repentance would bring him deliverance from his present suffering.
- His guilt had brought him divine wrath but his case was not hopeless. But earlier chapters have revealed that Job had not sinned before God.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Dear Lord, in this world it is difficult to be upright. Help me to be upright. In Jesus' name, Amen.


## Job 12 — Job Affirms God's Sovereign Power

### Summary

Job said, "Who does not know all these things? With God are wisdom and power. He brings darkness into light. He destroys nations."

### Key Lessons

**1. Job: Divine power**

- The final speech of the first cycle opens on a note of indignation at Job's friends (v 1-6).
- Job's sarcastic answer revealed his irritation at their doctrinaire condemnation of his character.
- Job seems to have emphasized in v 5 the friends' self-righteous attitude of complacency because danger can only befall people who are doomed to trouble anyway.
- Job continued to be preoccupied by life's injustice and observed that criminals prosper undisturbed (v 6). His friends joined in ridicule of the righteous person, Job, missing the fact that wickedness thrives in the world.
- The wicked thrive on power that comes from the same God whom the wicked defy.
- Having mocked his friends' naïve claim that the wicked always suffer and the righteous always prosper, Job now depicted a God entirely more unpredictable in his power than their reasoning allowed (v 13-25).
- Job's experience had no specific moral implication because God's power rules over all areas of our lives (v 16).

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our dear Heavenly Father, I thank you for loving us and for revealing that you rule over all areas of our lives. In Jesus' name. Amen


## Job 13 — Job Desires an Audience with God

### Summary

"I want to argue my case with God. Be quiet and I will speak. Though he slay me, I will hope in him. God, why do you hide your face?"

### Key Lessons

**1. Job prefers an audience with God**

- Beginning with v 1, Job's refuting of his friends' arguments included a protest that he did have wisdom, that he preferred an audience with God, and that his friends should be silent since God Himself would repudiate their fraudulent characterization of his person. Job recognized the risk of daring to discuss his case with the Almighty (v 14-15).

**2. I have no hope**

- The alternative reading I have no hope is not supported by v 15b,16: Job viewed God as his salvation and he was not a hypocrite.
- Job had certainly not given up on God. At this point Job's confidence in God's salvation and integrity still assured him of vindication before God.
- Job then launched into his most extended address to God (v 20-14:22). But his optimism would not remain constant, and gradually he focused less and less on speaking directly to God.
- His hope of swift physical restoration also seems to have faded. He spoke much less to God in the second and third cycles of speeches. By the second cycle his hope rested in seeing his redeemer in the future (19:25)

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our heavenly Father, in the darkness of life and the struggles we face each day, I do not want to lose my hope in you. Amen


## Job 14 — Job Reflects on Death and Hope

### Summary

"Man is like a fleeting shadow. If a tree is cut down, it will sprout again, but will a man live again? You overpower him forever."

### Key Lessons

**1. Second part of Job's address to God**

- Chapter 14 is the second part of Job's third address to God, introduced 13:28 as Job turned from defending his integrity against his friends to reflections on the finality of death.
- He wondered why God pays attention to finite humanity (v 3), something that may only increase their stress. He longed for God to let humanity rest (v 6).
- Humanity's fate is almost the most hopeless one within creation. Plants can spring up again but death is the end for humans (v 20,12) - at least "till heavens are no more" (v 12).
- Here in the verse is a hint (confirmed in other parts of scripture *(2 Peter 3:13; Revelation 21:1)* that the righteous dead would live again. The verses that follow (13-17) contains further hints of resurrection: Job spoke of being hidden in the grave until God's anger passed when he would be remembered (v 13).
- Within the overall argument about Job's integrity, he and Bildad engaged in the topic of sons. According to Bildad they had paid for their guilt (8:4). Job retorted that the doom he faced made the discussion of sons irrelevant (v 21).

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Dear Lord, thank you for the hope that we find in Jesus today, and also for the hope in the resurrection of the righteous dead. Amen


## Job 15 — Eliphaz Accuses Job of Arrogance

### Summary

Eliphaz said, "Your own mouth condemns you! Why do you turn against God? The wicked will be like a vine stripped of unripe grapes."

### Key Lessons

**1. Eliphaz: Learning and not Learning**

- Eliphaz was first indignant (v 1-6), then a borrower (v 7-10) and a little later an echo of himself (v 14-16), and finally he sought again to assert that the wicked always suffer (v 17-35).
- He suggested that Job should be silenced rather than increase his guilt by dishonest speech.
- He even asserted that Job was hindering piety by his words v 4. He accused Job of speaking from the perspective of his sinfulness and thus was expressing his lack of integrity.
- Eliphaz also implied that Job's show of emotion was misleading and alarming v 12.
- Eliphaz decided to return to his revelation v 14-16. He reiterated the point that human beings are sinful v 14. Verse 16 has been interpreted and translated to mean either that all human beings are abominable or detestable and filthy or corrupt or that Job himself was a particularly despicable example of human sinfulness.
- Verse 20 refers to the idea that the wicked or ruthless suffer according to the years appointed for them. This happens because they raise a rebellious hand against God v 25.
- The wicked are described as having fat faces (a symbol of prosperity); they appear to be thriving, but it ultimately proves futile.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Dear Lord, thank you for your word, may you help us to make decisions that please you always. In Jesus' name, Amen


## Job 16 — Job Calls His Friends Miserable Comforters

### Summary

Job said, "You're miserable comforters! God has torn me and shattered me but my prayer is pure. Even now, my advocate is on high!"

### Key Lessons

**1. Job Rebukes His Friends (v 1–5)**

- Job tells his friends that they are "miserable comforters" because their words have only increased his suffering. He says that if their roles were reversed, he would have encouraged and comforted them instead of accusing them.

**2. Job Maintains His Innocence (v 15–17)**

- Job mourns in humility, wearing sackcloth and weeping continually.
- Despite his suffering, he insists he has committed no violence and that his prayers have been sincere.
- He rejects the belief that his suffering proves hidden sin.

**3. Job Appeals to a Heavenly Witness (v 18–22)**

- Job asks that his innocence not be forgotten and expresses confidence that a witness in heaven knows the truth.
- Though he believes death is near, he hopes that God will eventually vindicate him.
- This cry lifts us up to the summit of his speech, v 19, in which the God who was Abel's witness was necessarily Job's own.
- The chapter highlights Job's deep anguish, unwavering claim of innocence and enduring hope that divine justice will ultimately prevail.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our dear heavenly Father, glory and honor be given to you for you are the true and faithful witness for the innocent. Help us to be upright in Jesus' name. Amen .


## Job 17 — Job's Hope Fades Toward the Grave

### Summary

"My spirit is broken. He has made me a byword. But come again all of you! I will not find a wise man among you. Where is my hope?"

### Key Lessons

**1. Job Feels Close to Death (Job 17:1–5)**

- Job declares that his spirit is broken and that he is nearing death. He feels surrounded by mockers whose accusations have deepened his suffering.
- He pleads with God to be his guarantor, believing that no one else will stand up for him.
- Job also warns that those who betray their friends for personal gain will face consequences.

**2. Job Rejects His Friends' Counsel (Job 17:10–12)**

- Job challenges his friends to continue speaking if they wish but he says none of them possesses true wisdom.
- He believes their advice has turned hope into despair by confusing darkness with light and offering no genuine comfort.

**3. Job Accepts the Nearness of Death (Job 17:13–16)**

- Job sees the grave as his future home and views death as his closest companion. He concludes that his earthly hopes have vanished and wonders who can still expect a better future for him.
- Job expresses profound despair as he anticipates descending to the grave, where all human hope seems to rest.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our dear heavenly Father, help us to know good and bad counsel. Above all, guide us to follow your word in Jesus' name. Amen.


## Job 18 — Bildad Describes the Fate of the Wicked

### Summary

**Bildad: A graphic terror story.** In terms of harshness, Bildad's second speech resembles language we associate with Zophar. But Bildad's rationale continued to depend on standard wisdom thinking.

### Key Lessons

**1. Bildad Rebukes Job (Job 18:1–4)**

- Bildad responds sharply to Job, accusing him of speaking without wisdom and treating his friends with contempt.
- He questions why Job believes others should change their understanding because of his complaints.

**2. The Fate of the Wicked (Job 18:5–10)**

- Bildad describes how the light of the wicked is extinguished. Their strength fades, their plans fail, and they are trapped by the very snares they have set, showing that evil ultimately leads to destruction.

**3. The Wicked Face Terror and Judgment (Job 18:11–21)**

- Bildad explains that fear follows the wicked wherever they go.
- Disease, calamity, and death consume them, while their memory and descendants disappear from the earth. Their homes become desolate, and future generations are astonished at their fate.
- Bildad concludes that such is the destiny of those who reject God, implying that Job's suffering is evidence of guilt rather than innocence.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our Father in heaven, help us to remain true to you when friends and family fail to understand our faith in you. In Jesus' name. Amen


## Job 19 — Job's Redeemer Lives

### Summary

This chapter records the briefest of Jobs speeches and the spiritual zenith of the book. From the depths of despair at God's treatment of him and the abandonment of servants, intimate friends and family, Job would soar to a testimony of confidence in his final vindication.

### Key Lessons

**1. Job Rebukes His Friends (Job 19:1–6)**

- Job responds by accusing his friends of tormenting him with repeated insults and false accusations.
- He insists that even if he has erred, his suffering is not proof of guilt. He believes God has allowed him to endure these hardships.

**2. Job Describes His Isolation (Job 19:7–22)**

- Job laments that God seems silent to his cries for justice. He feels trapped, stripped of honor and surrounded by suffering.
- His relatives, friends, servants and even his wife have abandoned him, leaving him completely alone. He pleads for compassion instead of condemnation.

**3. Job's Hope in His Redeemer (Job 19:23–29)**

- Despite his despair, Job expresses unwavering faith that his Redeemer lives and will ultimately vindicate him. He believes that even after death he will see God.
- Job warns his friends that they should fear God's judgment because they have condemned an innocent man.
- His declaration of faith stands as one of the strongest expressions of hope in the book.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our dear heavenly Father, in the middle of troubles, may I like Job declare that I know my redeemer lives. In Jesus' name. Amen.


## Job 20 — Zophar Warns of the Wicked's Brief Joy

### Summary

In this speech Zophar tried his hardest to frighten Job into repentance by showing him the horrible fate of the wicked.

### Key Lessons

**1. Zophar Responds to Job (Job 20:1–3)**

- Zophar answers Job with impatience, saying that Job's words have disturbed him.
- Confident in his understanding, he feels compelled to respond and defend his belief that God's justice is always immediate.

**2. The Short-Lived Success of the Wicked (Job 20:4–11)**

- Zophar argues that the prosperity of the wicked is brief. Though they may rise to great heights, they quickly perish and are forgotten. Their youthful strength fades and death overtakes them.

**3. The Certain Judgment of the Wicked (Job 20:12–29)**

- Using vivid imagery, Zophar explains that evil may seem sweet for a time but it eventually turns bitter and brings destruction.
- The wicked lose their wealth, experience God's wrath and cannot escape judgment.
- Their possessions vanish, their families suffer and disaster follows them.
- Zophar concludes that this is the portion God has appointed for the wicked, implying that Job's suffering proves he deserves divine punishment.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our Gracious Father in heaven, do not leave us alone. May your Holy Spirit guide and comfort us in the middle of life's challenges. In Jesus' name. Amen.


## Job 21 — Job Observes the Wicked Prosper

### Summary

Job paints his own very different picture of the life of the wicked as long, prosperous and secure. He asked rhetorically, his complaint was with other human beings and a negative answer was implied.

### Key Lessons

**1. Job Appeals for a Fair Hearing (v 1–6)**

- Job asks his friends to listen carefully before criticizing him. He explains that his complaint is directed toward God because of his suffering.
- He is overwhelmed with grief and amazement as he considers his circumstances and desires understanding rather than judgment.

**2. Job Questions the Prosperity of the Wicked (v 7–16)**

- Job challenges the belief that the wicked always suffer. He observes that many evil people live long, become wealthy, enjoy healthy families and experience peace and security.
- They prosper despite rejecting God and refusing to obey His ways, raising difficult questions about how divine justice operates.

**3. Job Refutes His Friends' Argument (v 17–34)**

- Job acknowledges that the wicked will ultimately face God's judgment but he argues that punishment is not always immediate or visible in this life.
- Both the righteous and the wicked die, regardless of their circumstances.
- He concludes that his friends' explanations are inadequate because they ignore the reality that earthly prosperity or suffering is not always a reliable measure of a person's relationship with God.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Dear Lord thank you for answering our questions through the book of Job. Help us to prepare for Judgement day. In Jesus' name Amen.


## Job 22 — Eliphaz's Final Accusations

### Summary

In this his final speech, Eliphaz spoke both his angriest and his most positive words to Job. His denunciations were fiercer, while his invitation was more promising.

### Key Lessons

**1. Eliphaz Accuses Job of Great Sin (v 1–11)**

- Eliphaz argues that God gains nothing from human righteousness and therefore would not punish an innocent person.
- He accuses Job of serious sins, including exploiting the poor, withholding food and water from the needy, sending widows away empty-handed and oppressing orphans. He claims these alleged sins explain Job's suffering.

**2. Eliphaz Declares God's Perfect Justice (v 12–20)**

- Eliphaz insists that God sees everything and judges the wicked. He reminds Job of the fate of evil people throughout history, arguing that those who rebel against God are ultimately destroyed by His judgment.

**3. Eliphaz Calls Job to Repent (v 21–30)**

- Eliphaz urges Job to be reconciled to God by repenting of his sins and turning away from wrongdoing.
- He promises that if Job returns to God, his prosperity will be restored, his prayers will be answered and he will once again experience peace, blessing and God's favor.

> 🙏 **Prayer:** Our heavenly Father, we now know that even the righteous suffer. Help us not to forsake you even in hard times. In Jesus' name. Amen.
